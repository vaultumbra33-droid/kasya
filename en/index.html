The problem

What ordinary apps know about you

Where you have been

Every photo with GPS carries the exact coordinates of where you took it, hidden inside: your home, your work, your children's school. Just sending it is enough.

What you took it with

Photos also store the date and time, your phone's make and model and, sometimes, the author's name.

What you said

Your messages pass through and stay on servers you do not control, and can live there long after you delete them.

Who you talk to

Even if the content is encrypted, who writes to you, when and for how long usually gets logged. That metadata tells your life story.

Who you are

An account tied to your phone number: your identity, handed over in advance, and often your contact list too.

Where you connect from

Your IP address reveals your city and your internet provider every time you connect to someone else's server.

What you like and what you fear

Profiles for advertising and analysis are built from your conversations and habits. Your daily life becomes a product.

Copies nobody told you about

Cloud backups, files that save themselves and photos that stay in the gallery with their location, even after you have sent them.

What happens if you are forced

If someone forces you to unlock your phone, your whole history is exposed. Almost no app has a plan for that moment.

You do not need to be a journalist or have something to hide. You just have to want what is yours to stay yours. KASYA was born to close exactly these doors.

What protects you

Built for when something goes wrong

Photos

Your photos travel without location

When you send a photo, KASYA removes the GPS position and the device and author data from the copy. The recipient sees the image, not where you took it. Your originals are not touched.

Messages

Disappearing messages

Choose how long a conversation should last and KASYA deletes the messages when the time is up. The timer is agreed on both sides of the chat.

Emergency

Emergency key

You set a second PIN. If someone forces you to open the app and you type that PIN, KASYA erases everything: accounts, messages, photos, documents and settings, and opens again as new. To anyone watching, it just restarted.

Access

Your app opens with your PIN

With a PIN, nobody gets in. Your phone being unlocked no longer means your chats are exposed.

Files you export yourself outside the app are not deleted by the emergency key. Disappearing messages depend on both people using KASYA.

Technology

How it works inside

KASYA is built on Jami, an open-source communication network. This is how a message of yours travels, without passing through anyone's server.

1. What P2P (peer-to-peer) is

In an ordinary app, every message goes through a central server: you talk to the company and the company talks to your contact. If that server goes down, slows down or is tampered with, everyone is affected.

In a P2P (peer-to-peer) network there is no center. Each phone is both client and server: it connects directly to the others. There is no single point to shut down, watch or sell.

  • No server that stores your messages
  • No single point of failure
  • No company that can read you or close your account

2. What a DHT is

A DHT (Distributed Hash Table) is like a giant address book spread across thousands of phones, where none of them has the complete copy.

  • Each piece of data has a key, obtained by applying a hash (a fingerprint) to your identifier.
  • The data is stored on the nodes whose identifier is closest to that key, and it is replicated on several in case one leaves.
  • To find something, each node brings you closer to the next nearest one: you arrive in a few hops, even if the network is huge.
  • Nobody is in charge: if a node disappears, the network rearranges itself.

Jami uses OpenDHT, a DHT with a design similar to Kademlia. On it, your phone publishes, signed, how to locate it, and your contact looks it up to find you.

  1. Step 1

    Your identity is born on your phone

    When you create your account, your cryptographic keys are generated on your device. There is no sign-up with phone or email: your identity is your key.

  2. Step 2

    Finding your contact

    Instead of a central server, a distributed network (OpenDHT) works as a directory among all participants. Nobody controls it and there is no single point to shut down.

  3. Step 3

    Direct connection

    The two phones connect to each other, getting through routers and networks with standard techniques (ICE, STUN and TURN). Your messages travel from one device to the other.

  4. Step 4

    End-to-end encryption

    Every conversation is encrypted with TLS and calls with SRTP. Only you and your contact have the keys to open what you say to each other.

  5. Step 5

    Conversations that sync by themselves

    Each chat is a "swarm": a shared history replicated across the participants' devices, with no master copy in the cloud.

  6. Step 6

    KASYA layers

    On top of that engine we add your PIN, the emergency key, disappearing messages, photos without location, channels and a dark, understated interface.

Glossary

The odd terms, explained

The underlined terms throughout the page lead here. Tap any of them to see its meaning.

APK
Installation file of an Android app. KASYA is distributed as an APK from this page, instead of the Play Store.
Channel
A KASYA space where only the administrator publishes and followers read.
End-to-end encryption
Only the sender and the receiver have the keys. Nobody in between, neither the network nor a server, can read the content.
Emergency key
A second PIN. If typed on the lock screen, KASYA erases everything stored in the app and reopens as new.
DHT (distributed hash table)
A directory spread across all the nodes of a network. Each piece of data has a key and is stored by the nodes closest to it; none of them has the complete directory.
EXIF
Information the camera stores inside the photo: date, device model and, if enabled, the GPS location. KASYA removes it from JPEG photos before sending them.
GPLv3
Free software license. It allows you to use, study, modify and share the program, and requires keeping those freedoms in derived versions.
Hash
Fixed-length fingerprint calculated from a piece of data. A tiny change in the data produces a completely different fingerprint, and it cannot be reversed.
ICE
Standard method for two devices behind routers to discover how to connect directly. It relies on STUN and TURN.
Jami
Free distributed communication project that KASYA is built on.
Kademlia
A widely used DHT design. Each node knows few distant neighbors and many close ones, which lets it find any data in a few hops.
Malware
Software made to damage the device, take control of it or steal information.
Disappearing messages
Messages that delete themselves when the chosen time is up. The timer is agreed on both sides of the chat.
Metadata
Data about data: who talked to whom, when, for how long and from where. Even if the message is encrypted, metadata can reveal a lot.
Node
Each device that takes part in a network. In a P2P network, your phone is a node.
OpenDHT
Free implementation of a DHT, used by Jami so that users can find each other without a central server.
P2P (peer-to-peer)
A network in which devices communicate directly with each other, with no intermediate server controlling the communication.
SHA-256
Hash function that produces a 256-bit fingerprint of a file. It is used to check that what you downloaded is identical to what was published.
Spyware
Software that secretly watches what you do and sends that information to others.
SRTP
Secure version of the real-time audio and video protocol. It encrypts and authenticates calls.
STUN
Service that tells a device its public address, so it can connect to another.
Swarm
History of a conversation that is replicated among its participants and synchronized with Git-like technology.
TLS
Connection encryption protocol, the same one that protects online banking.
TURN
Fallback server that relays traffic when a direct connection is impossible. The content stays encrypted.
VirusTotal
Free service that analyzes files with dozens of antivirus engines at once.
